Before the internet was opened to the public and before ".com" existed, almost no one knew whether — or how — the emerging network could serve teaching, research, and administration. I spent those years finding out: building one of the first educational gopher services, writing and speaking about how educators could actually use the internet, and translating a brand-new, highly technical capability for a non-technical national audience. That public body of work is what made me a known quantity in the earliest days of the web — and it's what led, in 1997, to Nike hiring me as its first webmaster.

What I built — the NWREL Internet Gopher

At the Northwest Regional Educational Laboratory I built and ran an Internet Gopher service that put educational research at educators' fingertips years before the graphical web made that ordinary. It offered searchable databases of Northwest educational projects and practices, NWREL's publications and newsletters, and regional conference listings — and it wired NWREL into the wider network: the other regional gophers across the five-state region, the ten regional educational laboratories nationally, and national services from the U.S. Office of Educational Research and Improvement (OERI) and the ERIC clearinghouses.

"With a few strokes of your computer keyboard, access to educational materials can be, literally, at your fingertips through NWREL's Internet Gopher. … Welcome to 'Gopherspace.'"
— from my NWREL publication, Educational Materials At Your Fingertips

Publishing & evangelizing the network

Building the service was only half the work; the other half was making it legible to people who had never heard the word "gopher." I wrote and published materials explaining the internet's availability and application for educators, parents, and policymakers — turning an unproven, jargon-heavy technology into something a classroom teacher could picture and use. This translate-for-non-experts instinct — adopt the new thing first, then teach everyone else to use it — became the throughline of my entire career, and it's exactly what AI enablement asks for today.

On the national stage

FARNET — "K-12 and the Internet" panel (fall 1994)

I moderated the "K-12 and the Internet" panel at a FARNET conference — credited as Kristin Boden-MacKay, Northwest Regional Educational Laboratory — on a program alongside people who were literally building the internet, including Mark McCahill, creator of the Gopher protocol, Clifford Neuman (USC/ISI), and Tom Longstaff (CERT / Carnegie Mellon). It is documented in the official Internet Monthly Report, November 1994 — a verifiable, primary-source record of my presence, at the internet's inception, among the people defining it.

Oregon Careers Conference (February 1995)

Recognized with a Certificate of Appreciation for presenting at the Oregon Careers Conference (Feb 17–18, 1995) — an early example of carrying this new capability to practitioners in the field.

The technical foundation

None of the evangelism was hand-waving — I ran the systems underneath it. In 1995 I completed formal Unix System Administration (Compatible Computers, Inc., April 25, 1995) and Solaris System Administration training, administering the servers and networks that made the gopher service and early web work. Real technical depth beneath the public-facing translation is a pattern that repeats straight through to architecting nike.com.

The line to Nike

By 1997, "person who has actually built internet services and can explain them to anyone" was a rare profile — and it was exactly the profile Nike needed to move a wholesale giant onto the web. The origin story and the nike.com story are one continuous line. See what came next →

Why it still matters. Very few people working in technology today were hands-on in the education/non-profit internet space before it went public. That depth — being first to a technology wave, then leading others onto it — is the same muscle now pointed at AI adoption. The names change (gopher, the web, Drupal, AI); the pattern doesn't.
